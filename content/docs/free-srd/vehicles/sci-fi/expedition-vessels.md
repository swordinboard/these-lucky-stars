# Expedition Vessels

Expedition ships are built around endurance rather than speed or plate. They go
somewhere nobody is, they stay there long enough to find out why, and they carry
everything that will be needed in the meantime — which is the entire reason
either of them is interesting at a table.

The two work as a pair, and are written that way: the explorer never touches a
surface, and the lander is how anybody gets to one.

## Choosing Between Them

**Survey Lander** is a party-sized ship and the one to reach for by default. Six
seats, a hold, weather it can actually fly in, and six weeks of air. It is
unarmed on purpose — a lander in trouble has its Speed line and nothing else,
and that constraint is most of what makes a survey scenario tense.

**Long-Range Explorer** is not transport, it is a location. Twelve crew, two
years out, and no possibility of setting down. Use it as the place a campaign
happens rather than as a thing the party drives: the hangar bay is the only door,
ten of its twelve compartments are unwatched at any hour, and help is measured in
months.

Running both, the failure state writes itself. Anything that happens to the
lander happens to the only way down, and anything that happens to the explorer
happens to the only way home.

## Encounter Notes

**Found** — Uncharted systems, resource surveys, salvage assessments, scientific
charters, and the last known position of an expedition that stopped reporting.

**Who Has Them** — Research institutes, prospecting concerns, and whichever
corporation is paying for a survey it intends to bury. Very few are privately
owned; almost all of them belong to somebody with an interest in the results.

**The Empty Ship** — An explorer with nobody aboard is the most reliable
adventure on this page. Two years of consumables means the crew did not starve,
the hangar bay tells the party whether the lander left, and every compartment is
a room somebody was in recently.
